    Glossary¶

    This glossary defines the key terms used across all six modules. Each entry includes a plain-language definition and a brief example. Terms are listed alphabetically. The Module tag tells you where the concept first appears.

    Use ++ctrl+f++ (or ++cmd+f++ on Mac) to search for a specific term.

    A¶

    Absolute Poverty¶

    A condition where a person's income or consumption falls below a minimum threshold needed to meet basic needs — food, shelter, clothing. Contrasted with relative poverty (being poor compared to others in your society). Example: The World Bank's international poverty line of $2.15/day (2017 PPP) is an absolute poverty measure. India's Tendulkar line serves a similar function domestically. Module 4

    Adverse Selection¶

    A situation where one party in a transaction has more information than the other, leading to a market where low-quality goods or high-risk individuals dominate. Example: If health insurance is voluntary, people who know they are sick are more likely to buy it, driving up premiums for everyone. Module 2

    Anchoring Bias¶

    The tendency to rely too heavily on the first piece of information encountered when making decisions. Example: A shop marks a shirt as "~~₹2,000~~ Now ₹999!" — the ₹2,000 anchor makes ₹999 feel like a bargain, even if the shirt was never worth ₹2,000. Module 1

    Asymmetric Information¶

    A situation where one party in a transaction knows more than the other. Example: A used car seller knows the car's history; the buyer does not. This information gap can lead to adverse selection and moral hazard. Module 2

    B¶

    Backward Induction¶

    A method of solving sequential games by starting at the final move and reasoning backwards to determine the optimal strategy at each stage. Example: In chess, you think several moves ahead by considering what your opponent will do at each future step. Module 2

    Behavioral Economics¶

    A field that combines insights from psychology and economics to study how people actually make decisions — often deviating from the "rational" predictions of standard economic theory. Example: People spend more using credit cards than cash because the pain of paying feels less immediate. Module 1

    Bounded Rationality¶

    The idea that people's decision-making is limited by the information available, their cognitive abilities, and the time they have to decide. Proposed by Herbert Simon. Example: Rather than comparing every mobile phone on the market, you buy the first one that meets your basic requirements. Module 1

    Budget Constraint¶

    The set of all combinations of goods and services a consumer can afford given their income and prices. Example: With ₹500 per week for food, you can buy 10 meals at ₹50 each, or 5 meals at ₹100, or any combination that totals ₹500. Module 2

    C¶

    Capital Deepening¶

    An increase in the amount of capital per worker in an economy. In the Solow model, capital deepening raises output per worker but faces diminishing returns. Example: Replacing hand looms with power looms in a textile factory — each worker now has more capital to work with, increasing their productivity. Module 5

    Cap-and-Trade¶

    Carbon Tax¶

    A tax levied on the carbon content of fossil fuels, designed to internalize the external cost of greenhouse gas emissions. A form of Pigouvian tax. Example: A carbon tax of $50 per ton of CO2 makes coal-fired electricity more expensive relative to solar, nudging the energy mix toward cleaner sources. Module 6 A market-based system for controlling pollution. The government sets a cap on total emissions and issues tradable permits. Firms that reduce pollution cheaply can sell their permits to firms that find it more expensive. Example: The EU Emissions Trading System covers power plants and factories across Europe. Module 6

    Capability Approach¶

    Amartya Sen's framework that measures well-being not by income alone but by what people are able to do and become — their "capabilities." Example: Two people may earn the same salary, but one lives in a village without schools or hospitals, limiting what they can achieve with that income. Module 4

    Child Labor¶

    The employment of children in work that deprives them of childhood, education, and well-being. Often driven by poverty and lack of alternatives. Example: India's Census 2011 recorded 10.1 million child laborers aged 5-14. The Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Amendment Act, 2016 prohibits employment of children below 14 in all occupations. Module 4

    Collective Action¶

    Common Pool Resource¶

    A resource that is rivalrous (one person's use reduces availability for others) but non-excludable (difficult to prevent access). Prone to overuse. Example: Groundwater — each farmer's borewell reduces the water table for all farmers, but no one can be easily excluded from drilling. India has 1,600+ over-exploited groundwater blocks. Module 6

    Commons Governance¶

    Systems of rules, norms, and institutions through which communities manage shared resources. Elinor Ostrom demonstrated that communities can govern commons sustainably without privatization or centralized control. Example: Van Panchayats in Uttarakhand manage ~5,400 sq km of community forests through locally designed rules about harvesting, monitoring, and sanctioning. Module 6 When individuals work together to achieve a common goal that benefits the group. Often difficult because of the free rider problem. Example: Villagers in Uttarakhand's Van Panchayats collectively manage forests, preventing overuse. Module 6

    Comparative Advantage¶

    The ability to produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than another producer. A country or person should specialize in what they do relatively best, even if they are not the absolute best at it. Example: India exports IT services not because Indian programmers are the cheapest, but because India's opportunity cost of producing software is lower than its opportunity cost of producing, say, aircraft. Module 3

    Competition¶

    A market structure where many firms sell similar products, and no single firm can control the price. Competition drives innovation and lowers prices. Example: The Indian telecom market after Jio's entry — intense competition drove data prices to among the lowest in the world. Module 2

    Complementary Goods¶

    Goods that are consumed together, so a rise in the price of one reduces demand for the other. Example: Petrol and cars — when petrol prices rise sharply, demand for fuel-inefficient cars falls. Module 3

    Consumer Surplus¶

    The difference between what consumers are willing to pay and what they actually pay. Example: You would pay ₹200 for a cup of coffee but it costs ₹100 — your consumer surplus is ₹100. Module 3

    Cooperation¶

    When players in a strategic interaction choose to work together rather than compete, often achieving better outcomes for all. Example: OPEC countries cooperate to limit oil production, keeping prices high — though each member is tempted to cheat. Module 2

    Creative Destruction¶

    Joseph Schumpeter's term for the process by which new innovations destroy old industries while creating new ones. Example: Smartphones destroyed the market for separate cameras, music players, and GPS devices — but created entirely new industries like app development. Module 5

    Credence Good¶

    A good or service whose quality the consumer cannot fully evaluate even after consumption. Example: You cannot independently verify whether your mechanic's repair was necessary or whether a doctor's prescription was optimal. Module 2

    D¶

    Deadweight Loss¶

    The loss of economic efficiency when the equilibrium outcome is not achieved — typically caused by taxes, subsidies, price controls, or monopoly. Example: A price ceiling on rent set below the market rate creates a shortage of apartments, and some willing renters and willing landlords cannot transact. Module 3

    Demand¶

    The quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to purchase at various prices, holding other factors constant. Example: At ₹50 per plate, 100 students buy biryani from the canteen; at ₹80, only 60 buy it. Module 3

    Diminishing Returns¶

    The general principle that increasing one input while holding others constant eventually yields progressively smaller increases in output. Applies broadly to production, investment, and growth. Example: In the Solow growth model, continued capital investment in a country yields smaller and smaller GDP gains — which is why technology, not just investment, drives long-run growth. Module 5

    Diminishing Marginal Returns¶

    The principle that adding more of one input (while holding others constant) eventually produces smaller and smaller increases in output. Example: Adding a second waiter to a small restaurant helps a lot; adding a tenth waiter to the same restaurant adds very little. Module 3

    Dominant Strategy¶

    A strategy that gives the best outcome for a player regardless of what the other player does. Example: In the Prisoner's Dilemma, confessing is a dominant strategy — it gives a better outcome whether the other prisoner confesses or stays silent. Module 2

    E¶

    Economic Growth¶

    A sustained increase in the total output (GDP) of an economy over time. Can be driven by more inputs (labor, capital) or better technology (TFP growth). Example: India's GDP grew at an average of ~7% per year from 2014-2024, driven by services expansion, demographic dividend, and infrastructure investment. Module 5

    Economic Mobility¶

    The ability of individuals or families to move up (or down) the income or social ladder over time. Example: A rickshaw driver's daughter becomes a software engineer — this represents upward intergenerational mobility. India's economic mobility is constrained by caste, gender, and geography. Module 4

    Economic Model¶

    A simplified representation of an economic process or relationship, used to make predictions and analyze policy. Models deliberately leave out details to focus on key mechanisms. Example: The supply-and-demand model assumes many buyers and sellers, but ignores the fact that real markets have advertising, brand loyalty, and information gaps. Module 1

    Economies of Scale¶

    Cost advantages that firms gain as production increases — the cost per unit falls as output rises. Example: Tata Motors can produce cars at a lower per-unit cost than a small workshop because it spreads fixed costs (factory, machinery) over millions of vehicles. Module 5

    Education Returns¶

    The economic gains from investing in education — measured as the percentage increase in earnings for each additional year of schooling. Also called "returns to education." Example: In India, completing secondary school increases average earnings by approximately 15-20% compared to primary school only, though returns vary by gender, caste, and region. Module 4

    Efficiency¶

    An allocation of resources where no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off (Pareto efficiency). Example: An economy operating on its Production Possibilities Frontier is productively efficient — it cannot produce more of one good without producing less of another. Module 1

    Elasticity¶

    A measure of how responsive quantity demanded or supplied is to a change in price or income. Example: Salt has low price elasticity (people buy roughly the same amount even if the price doubles); airline tickets have high elasticity (a small price drop can greatly increase bookings). Module 3

    Endowment Effect¶

    The tendency for people to value something more highly once they own it. Example: In Thaler's mug experiment, students who received a mug demanded ~$7 to sell it, while those who did not own one were willing to pay only ~$3. Module 1

    Equilibrium¶

    A state where no participant has an incentive to change their behavior, given what others are doing. In a market, it is the price where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. Example: If the market price of wheat is ₹25/kg, and at that price farmers want to sell exactly as much as consumers want to buy, the market is in equilibrium. Module 3

    Externality¶

    A cost or benefit of a transaction that affects a third party who did not choose to be involved. Example: A factory pollutes a river (negative externality), harming downstream farmers who had no say in the factory's production decisions. Module 6

    F¶

    Financial Crisis¶

    A sudden, widespread disruption of financial markets, typically involving bank failures, credit freezes, and sharp declines in asset prices. Example: The 2008 global financial crisis began with the collapse of the US housing bubble and spread through interconnected financial institutions worldwide. Module 1

    Fiscal Policy¶

    Government decisions about spending and taxation to influence the economy. Example: During the COVID-19 pandemic, the Indian government announced the Atmanirbhar Bharat package to boost economic activity through increased spending. Module 3

    Free Rider Problem¶

    When individuals benefit from a resource or service without paying for it, because they cannot be excluded. Example: If a neighborhood hires a private security guard, residents who do not contribute still benefit from the reduced crime. Module 6

    Framing Effect¶

    The tendency for people to react differently to the same information depending on how it is presented. Example: Patients are more likely to agree to surgery if told "90% survive" than if told "10% die" — the information is identical, but the frame changes the decision. Module 1

    G¶

    Free Rider¶

    A person who benefits from a shared resource or collective effort without contributing their fair share. The free rider problem makes it difficult for private markets to provide public goods. Example: A resident who enjoys the neighborhood park maintained by community donations but never donates themselves. Module 6

    Game Theory¶

    GDP Per Capita¶

    A country's total GDP divided by its population. A rough measure of average economic well-being, though it hides inequality. Example: India's GDP per capita in 2024 was approximately $2,700 — far below China ($13,000) or the US ($80,000), reflecting both India's large population and lower productivity. Module 5

    Gender Gap¶

    The systematic difference between men and women in economic outcomes — wages, labor force participation, education, or political representation. Example: India's female labor force participation rate is approximately 37% (PLFS 2024), compared to over 70% for men — one of the widest gender gaps in the world. Module 4

    Globalization¶

    The increasing integration of national economies through trade, investment, migration, and information flows. Example: An iPhone designed in California, assembled in China from components made in Japan, South Korea, and India, sold worldwide — this supply chain is globalization in action. India's IT exports ($200+ billion) are both a product and driver of globalization. Module 3 The study of strategic interactions where the outcome for each participant depends on the decisions of others. Example: Two competing telecom companies deciding whether to lower prices — each company's best move depends on what the other does. Module 2

    GDP (Gross Domestic Product)¶

    The total market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a given period. Example: India's GDP in 2024-25 was approximately $3.9 trillion, making it the world's fifth-largest economy. Module 5

    Gini Coefficient¶

    A measure of income inequality ranging from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (perfect inequality). Example: India's Gini coefficient is approximately 0.35, indicating moderate inequality — higher than many European countries but lower than Brazil or South Africa. Module 4

    H¶

    Herd Behavior¶

    When individuals follow the actions of a larger group, often ignoring their own information or analysis. Example: Investors piling into the stock market because "everyone else is making money" — a pattern that fuels market bubbles. Module 1

    Heuristics¶

    Mental shortcuts that people use to make decisions quickly. While often useful, heuristics can lead to systematic biases. Example: The "availability heuristic" — people overestimate the risk of plane crashes (dramatic, memorable events) and underestimate the risk of car accidents (common, unremarkable). Module 2

    Human Capital¶

    The skills, knowledge, and experience possessed by workers that make them productive. Investment in human capital (education, training, health) increases earning potential. Example: A software engineer who learns a new programming language increases their human capital and, typically, their salary. Module 5

    Human Development Index (HDI)¶

    A composite measure of well-being combining life expectancy, education, and income per capita. Created by the UNDP, based on Amartya Sen's capability approach. Example: Kerala ranks among India's highest HDI states despite modest per-capita income, because of strong performance in health and education. Module 4

    I¶

    Incentive¶

    A reward or penalty that motivates behavior. Economics assumes that people respond to incentives. Example: A congestion charge in central London incentivizes drivers to use public transport, reducing traffic. In India, MGNREGA's wage guarantee incentivizes rural workers to participate in public works. Module 1

    Inequality¶

    The uneven distribution of income, wealth, or opportunities across a population. Measured by the Gini coefficient, Palma ratio, or income share of the top/bottom groups. Example: India's top 10% hold approximately 57% of national income (World Inequality Lab), while the bottom 50% hold about 13%. Module 4

    Informal Economy¶

    Economic activity that operates outside formal regulations, taxation, and social protection systems. Includes self-employment, casual labor, and small unregistered enterprises. Example: Street vendors, domestic workers, and construction day-laborers — approximately 90% of India's workforce is informal, contributing roughly 50% of GDP. Module 4

    Income Elasticity of Demand¶

    A measure of how demand for a good changes when consumer income changes. Example: As Indian household incomes rise, demand for two-wheelers (normal goods) increases, while demand for kerosene (inferior good) decreases. Module 3

    Inferior Good¶

    A good for which demand decreases as consumer income rises. Example: Coarse grain — as incomes rise in rural India, families switch to rice or wheat. Module 3

    Information Asymmetry¶

    See Asymmetric Information. Module 2

    Innovation¶

    The introduction of new products, processes, or ideas that create value. Can be radical (entirely new) or incremental (improving existing products). Example: UPI was a radical innovation in digital payments; each successive version with new features represents incremental innovation. Module 5

    Institutions¶

    The formal rules (laws, regulations) and informal norms (customs, trust) that structure economic and social interactions. Example: Property rights, contract enforcement, and anti-corruption laws are institutions that determine whether markets function well. India's weak land title system is an institutional barrier to investment. Module 2

    L¶

    Labor Market¶

    The market where workers supply their labor and firms demand it. Wages are the "price" in this market. Example: India's labor market includes ~500 million workers, of whom approximately 90% are in the informal sector without written contracts or social security. Module 3

    Law of Demand¶

    All else equal, when the price of a good rises, the quantity demanded falls, and vice versa. Example: When Zomato offers a 50% discount, orders surge; when the discount is removed, orders fall. Module 3

    Law of Supply¶

    All else equal, when the price of a good rises, the quantity supplied rises, and vice versa. Example: When onion prices spike, more farmers rush to bring their stored onions to market. Module 3

    Loss Aversion¶

    The tendency for people to feel the pain of a loss more intensely than the pleasure of an equivalent gain — roughly twice as much. Example: Losing ₹100 feels worse than finding ₹100 feels good. This explains why investors hold losing stocks too long — selling would "realize" the loss. Module 1

    M¶

    Marginal Benefit¶

    The additional benefit gained from consuming one more unit of a good or service. Example: The first glass of water on a hot day is extremely valuable; the fifth glass is much less so. Module 1

    Marginal Cost¶

    The additional cost of producing one more unit of a good or service. Example: If a factory produces 100 shirts at ₹50,000 total and 101 shirts at ₹50,400 total, the marginal cost of the 101st shirt is ₹400. Module 1

    Marginal Thinking¶

    Making decisions by comparing the additional benefit of an action to its additional cost — one unit at a time. Example: "Should I study one more hour?" depends on whether the extra marks gained (marginal benefit) are worth the sleep lost (marginal cost). Module 1

    Market Bubble¶

    A situation where the price of an asset rises far above its fundamental value, driven by speculation and herd behavior, and eventually collapses. Example: The US housing bubble of 2003-2007, where house prices were driven up by speculative buying and easy credit, culminating in the 2008 financial crisis. Module 1

    Market Equilibrium¶

    See Equilibrium. Module 3

    Market Failure¶

    A situation where the free market does not allocate resources efficiently, justifying potential government intervention. Causes include externalities, public goods, monopoly, and information asymmetry. Example: Air pollution is a market failure because the polluter does not bear the full cost of the harm caused. Module 6

    Mental Accounting¶

    The tendency to treat money differently depending on where it comes from or how it is categorized, violating the economic principle that money is fungible. Example: Spending a tax refund on a luxury item ("bonus money") while simultaneously carrying credit card debt at 18% interest. Module 1

    Microfinance¶

    Small loans and financial services provided to low-income individuals who lack access to traditional banking. Example: Self-Help Groups (SHGs) in India pool savings and provide small loans to members, reaching over 100 million women. Module 4

    Monopoly¶

    A market structure with a single seller and no close substitutes. A monopolist can set prices above competitive levels. Example: Indian Railways is effectively a monopoly in long-distance rail transport — there is no competing rail service on the same routes. Module 3

    Moral Hazard¶

    When someone takes greater risks because they do not bear the full consequences of their actions. Example: Mortgage brokers in The Big Short issued risky loans because they earned commissions regardless of whether borrowers repaid — the risk was passed to investors. In India, the IL&FS crisis involved similar risk-shifting through the NBFC sector. Module 1

    Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI)¶

    A measure of poverty that considers multiple deprivations — health, education, and living standards — not just income. Example: According to NITI Aayog's 2023 report, India's MPI headcount ratio declined from 24.85% in 2015-16 to 14.96% in 2019-21. Module 4

    N¶

    Nash Equilibrium¶

    A set of strategies in a game where no player can improve their outcome by changing their strategy alone, given what the other players are doing. Named after John Nash. Example: In a duopoly where both firms charge moderate prices, neither firm benefits from unilaterally raising its price (losing customers) or lowering it (starting a price war). Module 2

    Natural Monopoly¶

    An industry where a single firm can supply the entire market at lower cost than two or more firms, typically due to high fixed costs. Example: Electricity distribution — it makes no sense to have two competing sets of power lines running to every home. Module 3

    Negative Externality¶

    A cost imposed on third parties who are not part of a transaction. Example: A coal power plant emits pollution that causes respiratory illness in nearby communities — the health costs are borne by residents, not the power company. Module 6

    Network Effects¶

    When the value of a product or service increases as more people use it. Example: WhatsApp is valuable because almost everyone in India uses it — a messaging app used by only 10 people has little value. Module 5

    Normal Good¶

    A good for which demand increases as consumer income rises. Example: Restaurant meals — as families earn more, they eat out more frequently. Module 3

    Normative Economics¶

    See Positive Economics. Module 1

    Nudge¶

    A small change in how choices are presented ("choice architecture") that steers people toward better decisions without restricting their freedom to choose otherwise. Coined by Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein. Example: Making organ donation the default option (opt-out rather than opt-in) dramatically increases donation rates. India's Swachh Bharat Mission used social norms messaging as a nudge. Module 1

    O¶

    Oligopoly¶

    A market dominated by a small number of large firms, where each firm's actions affect the others. Example: India's telecom market — dominated by Jio, Airtel, and Vodafone Idea — is an oligopoly where pricing and investment decisions are highly strategic. Module 3

    Opportunity Cost¶

    The value of the best alternative that you give up when you make a choice. The true cost of any decision. Example: If you spend Saturday studying instead of working a shift that pays ₹500, the opportunity cost of studying is ₹500 (assuming the job was your next-best option). Module 1

    P¶

    Pareto Efficiency¶

    See Efficiency. Module 1

    Perfect Competition¶

    A theoretical market structure with many small firms selling identical products, free entry and exit, and perfect information. No single firm can influence the market price. Example: Agricultural commodity markets approximate perfect competition — thousands of wheat farmers sell a homogeneous product and individually have no power over the price. Module 3 See Efficiency. Module 1

    Pigouvian Tax¶

    A tax on activities that generate negative externalities, designed to make the private cost equal the social cost. Named after economist Arthur Pigou. Example: A carbon tax that charges firms for each ton of CO2 emitted, internalizing the environmental cost. Module 6

    Platform Economy¶

    Economic activity organized through digital platforms that connect buyers and sellers, workers and firms, or users and content. Example: Swiggy, Uber, and Amazon are platforms — they do not produce the goods or services themselves but facilitate transactions between others. Module 5

    Positive Externality¶

    A benefit that accrues to third parties who are not involved in a transaction. Example: When you get vaccinated, you protect not only yourself but also people around you (herd immunity). Education also generates positive externalities — educated workers raise productivity for their colleagues and contribute to civic life. Module 6

    Positive Economics¶

    Price Mechanism¶

    The process by which prices coordinate the decisions of buyers and sellers in a market. Rising prices signal scarcity and encourage conservation; falling prices signal abundance and encourage consumption. Example: When onion prices spike due to a poor harvest, consumers reduce purchases and other farmers rush supply to market — the price mechanism coordinates these responses without any central planner. Module 3

    Private Good¶

    A good that is both excludable (non-payers can be prevented from consuming it) and rivalrous (one person's consumption reduces what is available for others). Most market goods are private goods. Example: A sandwich — if you eat it, no one else can, and the seller can refuse to give it to you unless you pay. Module 6 Economic statements about facts — claims that can be tested with evidence ("what is"). Contrasted with normative economics, which involves value judgments about "what ought to be." Example: "Raising the minimum wage to ₹300/day will reduce employment in small firms by 5%" is a positive statement (testable). "The minimum wage should be ₹300/day" is a normative statement (a value judgment). Module 1

    Poverty Line¶

    A threshold income or consumption level below which a person is classified as poor. Example: India's official poverty line (Tendulkar methodology) was set at ₹816/month in rural areas and ₹1,000/month in urban areas (2011-12 prices). Debate continues about whether these thresholds are adequate. Module 4

    Price Ceiling¶

    A legal maximum price that can be charged for a good or service. If set below the market equilibrium, it creates a shortage. Example: Rent control in Mumbai — by capping rents below market rates, the policy discourages new construction and creates a shortage of affordable housing. Module 3

    Price Floor¶

    A legal minimum price. If set above the market equilibrium, it creates a surplus. Example: India's Minimum Support Price (MSP) for wheat guarantees farmers a price above the market rate, encouraging production but sometimes creating government stockpile surpluses. Module 3

    Prisoner's Dilemma¶

    A game theory scenario where two rational individuals might not cooperate even when cooperation would benefit both, because each has an incentive to defect. Example: Two competing firms would both profit more by keeping prices high, but each is tempted to undercut the other — so both end up with low prices and low profits. Module 2

    Producer Surplus¶

    The difference between the price a producer receives and the minimum price they would accept. Example: A farmer willing to sell wheat at ₹20/kg but receiving the MSP of ₹25/kg earns a producer surplus of ₹5/kg. Module 3

    Production Possibilities Frontier¶

    A curve showing the maximum possible combinations of two goods an economy can produce with its available resources and technology. Points on the curve are efficient; points inside are inefficient; points outside are unattainable. Example: India's Union Budget illustrates the PPF — more spending on infrastructure means less available for subsidies, unless the economy grows. Module 1

    Productivity¶

    The amount of output produced per unit of input (labor, capital, or both). Higher productivity means more output from the same resources. Example: Mechanized farming produces more grain per worker-hour than manual farming. India's relatively low labor productivity is a major policy concern. Module 5

    Property Rights¶

    Legal rules that define who owns a resource and what they can do with it. Well-defined property rights are essential for markets to function. Example: Unclear land titles in rural India discourage investment because farmers cannot use their land as collateral for loans. Module 2

    Public Good¶

    A good that is non-excludable (you cannot prevent people from using it) and non-rivalrous (one person's use does not reduce availability for others). Example: National defense, street lighting, and clean air are public goods. Because people can free-ride, private markets typically under-provide them. Module 6

    R¶

    Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT)¶

    Regulation¶

    Government rules that constrain or direct economic behavior. Can address market failures (pollution standards, safety requirements) but can also create inefficiencies (excessive compliance costs, rent-seeking). Example: India's Bureau of Energy Efficiency star ratings for appliances — a regulation that uses information provision to steer consumer choices toward energy-efficient products. Module 6

    Relative Poverty¶

    Being poor relative to the standards of one's own society — typically defined as having income below a certain percentage (e.g., 60%) of the median. Contrasted with absolute poverty. Example: A family in Mumbai earning ₹15,000/month is above the absolute poverty line but may be in relative poverty given the high cost of urban living. Module 4

    Research and Development¶

    Systematic investment in creating new knowledge (research) and applying it to create new products or processes (development). A key driver of innovation and long-run growth. Example: India's R&D spending is approximately 0.7% of GDP — significantly below China (2.4%) and the US (3.5%), which partly explains the innovation gap. Module 5 An experimental method where participants are randomly assigned to a treatment group or a control group to measure the causal effect of an intervention. Example: Esther Duflo and Abhijit Banerjee used RCTs to test whether providing free bed nets (rather than selling them cheaply) increased malaria prevention in Africa. Module 4

    Rational Choice¶

    The assumption that people have clear preferences, weigh costs and benefits, and choose the option that maximizes their well-being. A foundational assumption of standard economics. Example: A rational consumer compares prices, quality, and alternatives before purchasing a phone. In practice, people often rely on brand loyalty, social proof, or impulse. Module 1

    Rent-Seeking¶

    Using resources to influence policy or regulations to gain economic advantage without creating new value. Example: A firm lobbying the government for a monopoly license rather than competing on quality and price. The "License Raj" era in India (pre-1991) was characterized by extensive rent-seeking. Module 3

    S¶

    Scarcity¶

    Shortage¶

    A situation where the quantity demanded exceeds the quantity supplied at the current price. Often caused by price ceilings or supply disruptions. Example: When the government caps the price of essential medicines below the market rate, pharmacies may run out of stock because suppliers find it unprofitable to sell at that price. Module 3

    Social Safety Net¶

    Government programs that provide a minimum level of support to vulnerable populations — through cash transfers, food subsidies, employment guarantees, or insurance. Example: India's social safety net includes MGNREGA (employment guarantee), PDS (food rations), PM-KISAN (cash transfers to farmers), and Ayushman Bharat (health insurance for 500 million poor). Module 4

    Solow Model¶

    See Solow Growth Model. Module 5 The fundamental economic problem: human wants are unlimited, but resources (time, money, land, labor, raw materials) are finite. Scarcity forces choices. Example: Even a billionaire faces scarcity of time. India faces scarcity of clean water despite being a large economy. Module 1

    Social Cost¶

    The total cost of an activity to society, including both private costs borne by the producer and external costs borne by third parties. Example: The social cost of driving includes the driver's fuel and time (private costs) plus pollution, congestion, and accident risk imposed on others (external costs). Module 6

    Solow Growth Model¶

    Robert Solow's framework showing that long-run economic growth depends primarily on technological progress, not just accumulating more capital or labor. Example: The model explains why simply building more factories (capital accumulation) eventually faces diminishing returns — sustained growth requires innovation. Module 5

    Strategic Interaction¶

    A situation where the outcome of your decision depends on the decisions made by others. The central subject of game theory. Example: A firm deciding whether to launch a new product must consider how competitors will respond — will they match the price, launch their own version, or ignore it? Module 2

    Subsidy¶

    A payment from the government to producers or consumers intended to encourage production or consumption of a good. Example: India's fertilizer subsidy makes agricultural inputs cheaper for farmers, but costs the government over ₹1.5 lakh crore annually and can distort market signals. Module 3

    Substitute Goods¶

    Goods that can replace each other, so a rise in the price of one increases demand for the other. Example: Tea and coffee — when coffee prices rise, some consumers switch to tea. Module 3

    Sunk Cost¶

    A cost that has already been incurred and cannot be recovered, regardless of future decisions. Rational decision-makers should ignore sunk costs. Example: You paid ₹2,000 for a non-refundable train ticket but get a better offer. The ₹2,000 is sunk — your decision should depend only on which option you prefer now, not on the money already spent. Module 1

    Surplus¶

    A situation where the quantity supplied exceeds the quantity demanded at the current price. Often caused by price floors. Example: When the government sets the Minimum Support Price for wheat above the market equilibrium, farmers produce more than consumers want to buy at that price, creating a surplus that the government must procure and store. Module 3

    Sustainability¶

    Meeting present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. Requires balancing economic growth with environmental preservation and social equity. Example: India's International Solar Alliance promotes sustainable energy transition — solar capacity grew from 2.6 GW in 2014 to 82+ GW in 2024, reducing dependence on coal without sacrificing growth. Module 6

    Supply¶

    The quantity of a good or service that producers are willing and able to sell at various prices, holding other factors constant. Example: At ₹80/kg, onion farmers bring 10,000 tonnes to market; at ₹40/kg, only 5,000 tonnes. Module 3

    Systemic Risk¶

    The risk that the failure of one institution or market triggers a cascade of failures across the entire financial system. Example: The collapse of Lehman Brothers in 2008 triggered a global credit freeze because Lehman was deeply interconnected with other financial institutions. India's IL&FS default in 2018 created similar contagion fears in the NBFC sector. Module 1

    T¶

    Technological Change¶

    Improvements in the methods or tools used to produce goods and services. Can be embodied (in new machines) or disembodied (new organizational methods). The primary driver of long-run economic growth in the Solow model. Example: The shift from manual record-keeping to computerized databases — the same number of clerks can process far more transactions. Module 5

    Technology Residual¶

    See Total Factor Productivity. Module 5

    Total Factor Productivity¶

    The portion of output growth that cannot be explained by increases in labor or capital inputs — often attributed to technological progress, efficiency improvements, or institutional quality. Also called the "Solow residual." Example: If India's GDP grows by 7% but labor and capital together explain only 4%, the remaining 3% is TFP growth. Module 5

    Trade-off¶

    The sacrifice of one thing in order to get something else. Every choice involves a trade-off because resources are scarce. Example: Studying an extra hour for your exam means one less hour of sleep — you trade rest for better preparation. Module 1

    Tragedy of the Commons¶

    The depletion of a shared resource when individuals act in their own self-interest without coordination. Example: Overfishing in the open ocean — each fishing boat takes as much as it can, and the collective result is collapse of fish stocks. India's groundwater crisis follows the same logic. Module 6

    U¶

    Unemployment¶

    The state of being without work while actively seeking employment. Can be frictional (between jobs), structural (skills mismatch), or cyclical (due to economic downturns). Example: India's PLFS 2024 reports an unemployment rate of approximately 3.2% — but this masks significant underemployment and disguised unemployment in the informal sector. Module 3

    Utility¶

    A measure of satisfaction or happiness that a consumer derives from consuming a good or service. Example: The first slice of pizza gives high utility; by the fourth slice, utility is much lower — illustrating diminishing marginal utility. Module 2

    V¶

    Value Added¶

    The increase in value that a firm contributes to a product at each stage of production. Example: A cotton farmer sells raw cotton for ₹50/kg. A spinning mill converts it to yarn worth ₹120/kg. The mill's value added is ₹70/kg. Module 5

    W¶

    Wage¶

    Wage Determination¶

    The process by which wages are set in a labor market — through supply and demand, collective bargaining, minimum wage laws, or employer market power. Example: In India's IT sector, wages are largely determined by market supply and demand (competitive labor market). In the organized manufacturing sector, union bargaining plays a larger role. MGNREGA sets a floor through its guaranteed wage. Module 3 The price of labor — the payment workers receive for their services. Determined by supply and demand in the labor market, influenced by skills, experience, bargaining power, and institutional factors like minimum wage laws. Example: MGNREGA guarantees a minimum wage (₹267-374/day depending on state) for unskilled manual labor in rural areas. Module 3

    Willingness to Pay¶

    The maximum amount a consumer would pay for a good or service. Example: If you would pay up to ₹150 for a movie ticket but the price is ₹100, your willingness to pay is ₹150 and your consumer surplus is ₹50. Module 3
