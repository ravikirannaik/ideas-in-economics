## Course Description

Economics is a way of understanding the world. This course uses films as a gateway to introduce fundamental economic concepts. Through movie analysis and Nobel Prize-winning research, students explore market behavior, incentives, labor dynamics, development, and policy. The course also serves as a foundation for further study in microeconomics and macroeconomics.

---

## Course Objectives

1. **Introduce core economic concepts** through accessible, real-world narratives drawn from cinema, enabling students to connect abstract ideas to concrete situations.
2. **Develop economic reasoning skills** by analyzing trade-offs, incentives, market behavior, and strategic interactions as depicted in feature films.
3. **Expose students to landmark economic research** by pairing each module with the work of a Nobel Prize-winning economist, grounding film-based discussions in rigorous scholarship.
4. **Apply economic thinking to the Indian context**, helping students understand how concepts like informality, public goods, labor markets, and development policy operate in India's economic landscape.

## Suggested Pedagogy

This course adopts an interactive, film-based pedagogical approach. Each module begins with a feature film screening (or assigned viewing) that introduces students to the module's economic themes through narrative. Classroom sessions then unpack the economic concepts embedded in the film, using guided discussion, worked examples, and short problem-solving exercises.

Nobel Prize-winning research is woven into each module to connect popular narratives with frontier scholarship. Interactive browser-based simulations allow students to experiment with economic models --- supply and demand, externalities, game theory --- in real time. Indian Context essays and case studies ground the material in the local economic environment, ensuring relevance beyond the textbook.

The assessment design emphasizes progressive engagement: problem sets build analytical skills throughout the semester, the mid-term tests conceptual mastery of the first half, and the end-term empirical project requires students to synthesize the full course into an original, evidence-based analysis.
