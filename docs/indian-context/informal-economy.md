# India's Informal Economy: The Invisible Majority

## Overview

Walk through any Indian city and the informal economy surrounds you before you notice it. The vegetable vendor on the footpath, the auto-rickshaw driver without a fixed salary, the construction worker hired for a day, the woman stitching garments in her one-room home — none of them have a written employment contract, employer-provided health insurance, or a pension. They are part of the informal economy, and in India, they are not the exception. They are the overwhelming majority.

According to the Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS) 2023-24, roughly 89% of Indian workers are employed in the informal sector or hold informal jobs within formal enterprises. This is not a fringe phenomenon or a transitional phase that will vanish with economic growth. It is the dominant mode of economic life for nearly 500 million working Indians. Understanding the informal economy is essential to understanding India itself — its patterns of scarcity, its labor markets, and its persistent poverty.

The informal economy also challenges some of the neat models you learn in economics textbooks. Standard supply-and-demand analysis assumes well-defined markets with clear contracts, enforceable property rights, and reliable information. In informal India, none of these hold perfectly. Workers bargain without contracts. Firms operate without registration. Transactions rely on trust, reputation, and social networks rather than legal enforcement. This does not mean economics fails here — it means economics gets far more interesting.

## Who Are the Informal Workers?

India's informal workforce is not a homogeneous mass. It spans at least three broad categories, each with distinct economic characteristics.

**Self-employed workers** form the largest group — about 57% of all workers according to PLFS 2023-24. This includes own-account workers (a carpenter who finds his own customers), employers of small household enterprises (a woman running a street-food stall with a helper), and unpaid family workers (a farmer's daughter who works the land without receiving a separate wage). Self-employment in India is often less a sign of entrepreneurial ambition and more a reflection of the absence of better options — what economists call "disguised unemployment."

**Casual laborers** make up roughly 21% of the workforce. These are workers hired on a daily or piece-rate basis with no guarantee of employment tomorrow. Construction sites across India run on casual labor. A worker shows up at a *naka* (labor gathering point) each morning, negotiates a daily wage, and works until sundown. No work means no income — there is no paid leave, no sick days, no severance.

**Home-based workers** are perhaps the most invisible of all. Millions of women across India — estimates range from 5 to 12 million — work from home producing garments, bidi cigarettes, incense sticks, and electronic components. They are at the bottom of complex supply chains that connect a woman rolling bidis in a village in Madhya Pradesh to a wholesale distributor in Mumbai. Their contribution to GDP is real but rarely counted accurately.

!!! info "Data Point"
    The National Statistical Office estimates that the informal sector contributes approximately 50% of India's GDP. Yet its workers receive a disproportionately small share of national income, reflecting the low productivity, low wages, and near-zero bargaining power that characterize informal work.

## Dharavi: A Microcosm of Informal Enterprise

No discussion of India's informal economy is complete without Dharavi, the densely packed neighborhood in Mumbai that has become a global symbol of informal enterprise. Spread across just 2.4 square kilometers, Dharavi is home to an estimated 600,000 to 1 million residents and generates an estimated $1 billion or more in annual economic output.

Dharavi is not simply a slum to be pitied. It is a remarkably productive informal industrial district. Leather goods, recycled plastics, pottery, textiles, and food processing all thrive here, organized into micro-clusters where tiny workshops of 5-10 workers specialize in one part of a production chain. A recycling unit melts plastic waste into pellets, which another workshop moulds into consumer products, which a third unit packages and sells to retailers across India. The division of labor is sophisticated; the technology is basic; the scale is enormous.

Yet Dharavi also illustrates the limits of informality. Without formal property rights, residents cannot use their dwellings as collateral for bank loans. Without environmental regulation, toxic fumes from recycling units mix with residential air. Without labor protections, a worker who loses a finger on a plastic shredder has no compensation and no recourse. Dharavi's productivity is real, but so is its precariousness.

If you watch *Slumdog Millionaire* in Module 4, keep Dharavi in mind. The film dramatizes the lives of children growing up in Mumbai's informal settlements, but the economics underneath the drama — lack of formal property rights, absence of social safety nets, reliance on informal networks — is not fiction. It is the daily reality of informal India.

## Why Does Informality Persist?

Economists have long debated why informality remains so pervasive in developing countries despite decades of economic growth. Three explanations carry the most weight in India's case.

**First, the cost of formality is high.** Registering a business, obtaining licenses, filing tax returns, and complying with labor laws impose fixed costs that a small enterprise — a tea stall, a tailoring unit, a vegetable cart — simply cannot afford. The World Bank's Doing Business surveys repeatedly showed that India's regulatory burden was among the heaviest in the world. For a tiny firm, staying informal is not evasion; it is rational economic behavior.

**Second, the formal sector does not generate enough jobs.** India's organized manufacturing sector employs only about 14% of the workforce. The boom sectors of the last two decades — IT services, finance, telecommunications — are skill-intensive and absorb a relatively small share of the labor force. For the hundreds of millions of Indians with limited formal education, the informal sector is not a choice but the only available option.

**Third, social structures channel workers into informal occupations.** Caste, gender, and rural-urban divides shape who gets access to formal employment. Women's labor force participation in India is among the lowest in the world — around 37% according to PLFS 2023-24 — and women who do work are disproportionately concentrated in informal, home-based, and unpaid family work.

## E-Shram and the Social Security Challenge

In 2021, the Indian government launched the **e-Shram portal**, a national database for unorganized workers. The goal was straightforward but ambitious: create a registry of informal workers so that the government could extend social security benefits — accident insurance, pension schemes, maternity benefits — to workers who had never been covered.

By 2024, over 30 crore (300 million) workers had registered on e-Shram, making it one of the largest databases of informal workers in the world. The portal captures basic information: name, occupation, Aadhaar number, bank account, skill type.

The challenge, however, is that registration is not the same as coverage. Many workers who registered on e-Shram have yet to receive any tangible benefits. The Pradhan Mantri Shram Yogi Maan-dhan (PM-SYM) pension scheme for informal workers, launched in 2019, had enrolled only about 47 lakh (4.7 million) workers by 2024 — a fraction of the eligible population. The gap between creating a database and delivering universal social protection remains vast.

!!! warning "The Gap"
    India spends approximately 1.3% of GDP on social protection (excluding food and fuel subsidies), compared to a world average of about 12.9%. For the roughly 450 million informal workers, the safety net remains thin: no unemployment insurance, no employer-provided health coverage, and pension coverage that reaches only a small minority.

## Module Connections

!!! example "Module Connection: Scarcity (Module 1)"
    The informal economy is where scarcity is most visible and most consequential. When a casual laborer earns only on days when work is available, every decision — whether to spend on food or medicine, whether to send a child to school or to work — involves trade-offs that are immediate and stark. The Production Possibilities Frontier is not an abstract diagram for these families; it is lived experience.

!!! example "Module Connection: Labor Markets (Module 3)"
    Informal labor markets violate several assumptions of standard labor-market models. There is no minimum wage enforcement, no employer-employee contract, and limited information on both sides. Wages are set through daily bargaining rather than market equilibrium. Studying the informal sector reveals what labor markets look like when institutions — contracts, courts, unions — are absent.

!!! example "Module Connection: Poverty and Inequality (Module 4)"
    The link between informality and poverty is not coincidental. Informal workers earn less, save less, and are more vulnerable to shocks — illness, crop failure, economic downturns. Esther Duflo and Abhijit Banerjee's research, which you study in Module 4, directly engages with the question of why the poor make the economic decisions they do. Many of those decisions are made within the constraints of informal employment.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Approximately 89% of India's workers are in informal employment** — without written contracts, social security, or labor law protection. This is the norm, not the exception.
2. **The informal sector contributes about half of GDP** but offers low wages, zero job security, and no institutional safety net.
3. **Informality persists because the costs of formality are high, formal jobs are scarce, and social structures limit access** to organized-sector employment.
4. **Dharavi exemplifies both the productivity and the precariousness** of informal enterprise — sophisticated division of labor alongside absent property rights and environmental regulation.
5. **E-Shram registered 300 million workers**, but the gap between registration and meaningful social protection remains India's largest unfinished policy challenge.
