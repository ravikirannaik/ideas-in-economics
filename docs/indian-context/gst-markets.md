# GST: One Nation, One Market?

## Overview

Imagine you are a truck driver hauling a shipment of auto parts from a factory in Chennai to a warehouse in Delhi — a distance of about 2,200 kilometers. Before July 2017, your journey would have been interrupted at nearly every state border. At the Karnataka-Andhra Pradesh border, you wait for hours while officials check your papers and collect **entry tax**. At the Maharashtra border, you pay **octroi** — a medieval-sounding levy that cities charged on goods entering their jurisdiction. In Madhya Pradesh, another form of local tax. Each state has its own **Value Added Tax (VAT)** with different rates, different forms, different exemptions. By the time you reach Delhi, you have paid four or five different taxes to four or five different authorities, spent 30-40% of your total journey time waiting at checkpoints, and generated a stack of paperwork that no single system can track.

This was not a hypothetical. A World Bank study estimated that Indian trucks spent 60% of their time **not moving** — waiting at toll plazas, state borders, and tax checkpoints. The average speed of freight trucks in India was about 20-25 km/hour, compared to 60-80 km/hour in developed countries. The economic cost was enormous: inefficient logistics added an estimated 13-14% of GDP in logistics costs, compared to 8-10% in China and 7-8% in developed economies.

On July 1, 2017, India replaced this patchwork with a single, unified tax: the **Goods and Services Tax (GST)**. It was the most significant tax reform since independence — a constitutional amendment (the 101st) that fundamentally restructured the fiscal relationship between the Centre and the states. The promise was audacious: One Nation, One Tax, One Market.

## The Pre-GST Problem: Fragmentation as a Tax on Growth

To understand why GST mattered, you need to understand what came before. India's pre-GST indirect tax system was a product of history, federalism, and political compromise. The **Centre** levied excise duty on manufacturing and service tax on services. The **states** levied VAT on sales, entry tax on goods crossing state borders, entertainment tax, luxury tax, and a dozen other levies. **Local bodies** added octroi and local body tax (LBT) in some states.

The result was a **cascading** tax system — a tax on a tax. When a manufacturer paid excise duty on raw materials, that cost was embedded in the price. The next buyer paid VAT on the entire price, including the excise component. The tax accumulated at every stage, raising final prices without adding value. Economists call this **cascading** or **tax-on-tax**, and it distorts economic decisions: firms vertically integrate (do everything in-house) not because it is efficient but because buying from outside suppliers means paying more taxes.

Cascading also created **tax arbitrage**. Companies located warehouses not where logistics were most efficient but in states where tax rates were lowest. Inter-state trade was discouraged because it triggered additional Central Sales Tax (CST) that was not fully creditable. The system, in effect, **taxed the act of doing business across state lines** — fragmenting what should have been a single national market into 29 separate state-level markets (now 28 states and 8 Union Territories).

!!! info "Data Point"
    Before GST, India had over 17 different indirect taxes levied by Centre, states, and local bodies. A business selling goods across all states potentially had to comply with dozens of different tax laws, file hundreds of returns annually, and maintain separate accounts for each jurisdiction.

## How GST Works: The Four-Tier Structure

GST is a **destination-based, multi-stage tax with input tax credit**. Let us unpack each element.

**Destination-based** means the tax revenue goes to the state where the good or service is **consumed**, not where it is produced. This was a major shift: under the old system, manufacturing states like Gujarat and Maharashtra earned disproportionate tax revenue while consuming states in eastern India received less. GST corrected this imbalance, though it required a complex **compensation mechanism** to protect states from revenue loss during the transition.

**Multi-stage** means GST is collected at every point in the supply chain — manufacturing, wholesale, retail — but **input tax credit** ensures that each stage pays tax only on the **value it adds**, not on the full price. If a manufacturer pays Rs 18 GST on raw materials worth Rs 100, and sells the finished product for Rs 200, the manufacturer charges Rs 36 GST on the sale but claims credit for the Rs 18 already paid. The effective tax is only on the Rs 100 of value added. This eliminates cascading.

The **four-tier rate structure** is: 5% (essential goods like packaged food, basic clothing), 12% (processed food, business-class air tickets), 18% (the standard rate covering most goods and services), and 28% (luxury and sin goods like automobiles, aerated drinks, tobacco). Some items — unbranded food grains, fresh vegetables, milk — are exempt (0%).

The system is administered jointly by the Centre and states through the **GST Council**, a constitutional body where the Union Finance Minister chairs and all state finance ministers vote. The council decides rates, exemptions, thresholds, and procedural rules. It is a remarkable experiment in **cooperative federalism** — states voluntarily gave up their independent power to tax in exchange for a share of a common pool.

## What Changed: The Economic Impact

The effects of GST on India's economy have been substantial, though uneven.

**Logistics and market integration.** The abolition of inter-state checkpoints and entry taxes had an immediate impact. The average trip time for trucks between Delhi and Mumbai fell by 18-20% in the first two years after GST, according to data from Rivigo, a logistics company. Warehousing decisions shifted from tax optimization to logistical efficiency — companies consolidated warehouses, reducing inventory costs. The e-way bill system, introduced in 2018, digitized the tracking of goods movement, replacing the paper permits that truck drivers used to carry in bundles.

**Formalization.** GST's input tax credit mechanism created a powerful incentive for firms to deal with registered suppliers — because you can only claim credit on taxes paid to GST-registered vendors. This pulled firms into the formal tax net. The number of GST registrations grew from about 66 lakh (6.6 million) at launch to over 1.4 crore (14 million) by 2024. Monthly GST collections crossed Rs 1.87 lakh crore in April 2024, more than double the initial collections.

**Revenue buoyancy.** After early teething troubles — the first year saw collections well below projections — GST revenue has grown consistently. The broader tax base (more registered firms, better compliance tracking through data analytics) has partially compensated for the lower average rate compared to the pre-GST regime.

!!! tip "Key Insight"
    GST is fundamentally about reducing **transaction costs** — the concept from Module 3. Every checkpoint eliminated, every form removed, every hour of waiting time saved is a reduction in the cost of doing business. When transaction costs fall, markets expand, specialization increases, and the economy moves closer to its production possibilities frontier.

## Challenges: Why "One Nation, One Market" Remains Incomplete

GST was necessary. Whether it was sufficient is a different question. Several significant challenges remain.

**Rate complexity.** The original vision was a single rate (or at most two rates). What India got was four rates plus a compensation cess plus dozens of exemptions. A biscuit priced under Rs 100 is taxed at 12%; above Rs 100, at 18%. Handmade and machine-made distinctions, brand-name versus unbranded goods, composition schemes for small firms — the classification complexity has generated a huge volume of litigation. The GST Appellate Tribunals, meant to resolve disputes quickly, were constituted only in 2023, six years after launch.

**Compliance burden on small firms.** For a large corporation with ERP systems and tax consultants, GST compliance is manageable. For a small shopkeeper or artisan, filing monthly or quarterly returns on the GST Network (GSTN) portal is a significant burden. The system requires digital literacy, internet access, and an understanding of input tax credit mechanics that many small business owners lack. The composition scheme (a simplified flat-rate option for firms with turnover below Rs 1.5 crore) helps but covers only a fraction of small businesses.

**The compensation dispute.** The Centre promised to compensate states for any revenue shortfall below 14% annual growth for the first five years (2017-2022). When GST collections fell short — especially during COVID-19 — the compensation fund ran dry, and the Centre and states clashed over who should borrow to fill the gap. The compensation period has ended, and several states now face revenue pressures, raising questions about the long-term fiscal sustainability of the GST architecture.

**Exclusions.** Petroleum products, electricity, alcohol, and real estate remain partially or fully outside GST. These are among the highest-revenue items for state governments, and states have resisted bringing them in. As long as these major sectors are excluded, the "One Nation, One Market" vision remains incomplete.

## Key Takeaways

1. **GST replaced 17+ indirect taxes** with a single, unified tax, constitutionally restructuring Centre-state fiscal relations.
2. **The elimination of inter-state checkpoints reduced truck transit times by 18-20%** and began shifting warehousing decisions from tax optimization to logistical efficiency.
3. **Input tax credit eliminates cascading** — each firm pays tax only on value added, not on the full price including embedded taxes from earlier stages.
4. **Monthly collections have more than doubled** since launch, reflecting a broader tax base and improved compliance, with over 14 million registered businesses.
5. **Rate complexity, compliance burden on small firms, and the exclusion of petroleum and alcohol** mean that "One Nation, One Market" remains a work in progress rather than a completed achievement.
