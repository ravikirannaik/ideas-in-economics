# Governing India's Commons: From Chipko to the International Solar Alliance

## Overview

In 1968, the ecologist Garrett Hardin published a famous essay called "The Tragedy of the Commons." His argument was simple and influential: when a resource is shared by many people and owned by none — a common grazing pasture, a fishery, the atmosphere — each individual has an incentive to overuse it. The herder adds one more cow to the common pasture because the benefit (more milk) goes entirely to him while the cost (overgrazing) is shared among all herders. Multiply this logic across every user, and the commons is destroyed. Hardin's conclusion was stark: shared resources must either be **privatized** (give each herder their own plot) or controlled by a **centralized authority** (the government decides how many cows each herder can graze).

But is this really the only choice — private ownership or government control? In 2009, Elinor Ostrom won the Nobel Prize in Economics for demonstrating that it is not. Across the world, she documented communities that had successfully managed shared resources for generations — without privatization and without a central authority — through local rules, monitoring, and graduated sanctions. Ostrom showed that people are capable of governing the commons themselves, provided certain institutional conditions are met.

India offers some of the world's most compelling examples of both the tragedy of the commons and its solutions. This essay traces the arc from community forest management in the hills of Uttarakhand to the Chipko movement's collective action, from Joint Forest Management policies to India's emergence as a leader in the global climate commons through the International Solar Alliance. Together, these cases show that governing the commons is neither a theoretical puzzle nor a lost cause — it is an ongoing, practical challenge that Indians have been grappling with for centuries.

## Van Panchayats: Community Forestry in Uttarakhand

Long before Ostrom's academic work, the hill communities of what is now Uttarakhand had their own system for managing common forests. The **Van Panchayats** (forest councils) of the Kumaon region, formally constituted under the Van Panchayat Rules of 1931 but rooted in much older customary practices, are among the oldest community forest governance institutions in the world.

A Van Panchayat is a locally elected body that manages a designated forest area on behalf of the village. The council sets rules: which trees can be harvested and when, how much fodder each household can collect, when the forest is closed for regeneration, and what penalties apply for violations. The rules are not imposed from above — they are decided by the community and enforced through social pressure, fines, and, in serious cases, exclusion from forest benefits.

Today, there are approximately 12,000 Van Panchayats in Uttarakhand managing over 5,400 square kilometers of forest — about 14% of the state's forest area. Research by environmental economists Arun Agrawal and Elinor Ostrom herself has shown that Van Panchayat forests are, on average, better maintained than state-managed forests in comparable ecological conditions. Community monitoring is more consistent, illegal felling is caught faster, and regeneration rates are higher.

The secret is not that village communities are inherently virtuous. It is that the institutional design aligns incentives. Villagers who depend on the forest for fuel, fodder, and timber have a **long-term stake** in its survival. The forest is not an abstraction — it is the source of daily cooking fuel and cattle feed. Rules are made by those who live with the consequences. And violators are sanctioned by people they see every day, making the social cost of cheating high.

!!! info "Data Point"
    Uttarakhand's Van Panchayats manage roughly 14% of the state's forest area with minimal government funding. Studies comparing Van Panchayat forests with adjacent reserve forests (managed by the Forest Department) find 15-20% better canopy cover in community-managed areas, suggesting that local governance outperforms centralized management for these mid-altitude forests.

## Chipko: Collective Action in the Mountains

In March 1973, in the village of Mandal in the Chamoli district of what was then Uttar Pradesh (now Uttarakhand), a group of villagers led by Chandi Prasad Bhatt did something that would become iconic: they hugged trees to prevent a logging contractor from felling them. The **Chipko movement** (from *chipko*, meaning "to cling" or "to embrace") had begun.

The immediate trigger was the allocation of ash trees to a sports goods manufacturer from the plains, even as the village's request for a few trees to make agricultural implements had been denied by the forest department. The deeper cause was decades of commercial logging that stripped the Himalayan forests — causing landslides, drying up streams, and destroying the subsistence base of hill communities — while the profits went to contractors and the state government.

Chipko was not a single event but a cascade of resistance that spread across the Garhwal and Kumaon hills between 1973 and 1981. Women played a central role — most famously in the 1974 Reni episode, where Gaura Devi led village women to confront loggers when the men were away. The movement used Gandhian non-violent methods and drew on traditional ecological knowledge, but it also made sharp economic arguments: the forests were a **common-pool resource** whose benefits (water, soil stability, biodiversity, subsistence products) accrued to local communities, while the costs of deforestation (floods, erosion, loss of livelihood) were borne by those same communities.

The economic impact was significant. In 1980, the Government of India imposed a 15-year ban on commercial green felling above 1,000 meters in the Himalayan region — a direct response to Chipko. The movement also influenced the **Forest Conservation Act of 1980**, which required central government approval for diverting forest land to non-forest uses. Chipko demonstrated that collective action by communities can change national policy, even when those communities have no formal political power.

!!! example "Module Connection: Collective Action (Module 6)"
    Chipko is a textbook case of the **collective action problem**. Each individual villager benefits from the forest, but organizing resistance is costly — it takes time, risks confrontation with contractors and officials, and any single person could free-ride on others' efforts. Chipko succeeded because community bonds (shared dependence on the forest, Gandhian moral framing, women's leadership) overcame the free-rider problem. Ostrom's later academic work identified exactly these features — face-to-face communication, shared dependence, graduated sanctions — as the institutional conditions that make collective action possible.

## Joint Forest Management: The Policy Response

Chipko and similar movements across India prompted a rethink of top-down forest governance. In 1990, the Government of India issued a circular encouraging **Joint Forest Management (JFM)** — a partnership between the Forest Department and local communities for managing degraded forest lands.

Under JFM, a **Village Forest Protection Committee (VFPC)** or similar body is formed with representation from the Forest Department and the village community. The community undertakes to protect and regenerate the forest; in return, it receives a share of forest products (non-timber forest products like tendu leaves, honey, and medicinal plants) and, in some states, a portion of timber revenue.

By 2024, JFM programs covered approximately 28 million hectares across 29 states, involving over 1.2 lakh (120,000) community committees. The results are mixed but instructive. In states like West Bengal and Odisha, where implementation was genuine and communities had real decision-making authority, forest regeneration has been measurable — satellite data shows increased canopy density in JFM areas compared to baseline. In other states, JFM remained a top-down formality: the Forest Department retained effective control, community participation was tokenistic, and the promised revenue-sharing never materialized.

The lesson from JFM echoes Ostrom's framework: **community governance of commons works when communities have genuine authority, clear boundaries, and a real stake in the outcomes.** When "joint management" is merely a label on a government program that gives communities responsibilities without rights, it fails.

## India's Water Commons

If forests are India's most studied commons, **water** is its most contested. India has approximately 18% of the world's population but only 4% of its freshwater resources. The Central Ground Water Board estimates that over 1,600 blocks across India are classified as "over-exploited" — groundwater is being extracted faster than it is recharged.

The tragedy of the commons plays out visibly in India's groundwater crisis. When a farmer drills a borewell, the cost (electricity, drilling) is private but the aquifer is shared. Each farmer has an incentive to drill deeper and pump more, because the water he does not extract will be extracted by his neighbor. The result is a race to the bottom — literally. Well depths in parts of Punjab, Haryana, and Tamil Nadu have increased from 10-15 meters in the 1970s to over 100 meters today. As aquifers deplete, the marginal farmer who cannot afford a deeper borewell loses access entirely.

Yet India also has traditions of **community water management** that predate the groundwater crisis. The **Johads** (earthen check dams) of Rajasthan, revived by Rajendra Singh and the Tarun Bharat Sangh in the 1980s, are a celebrated example. By building and maintaining johads through village labor and collective decision-making, communities in the Alwar district recharged groundwater, revived seasonal rivers, and restored agricultural productivity — without any government funding.

These water commons solutions work at the village scale. The challenge is scaling them. India's groundwater governance remains fragmented — the Easement Act of 1882 gives landowners unrestricted right to groundwater beneath their property, a legal framework that essentially treats a common-pool resource as private property and all but guarantees overexploitation.

## The International Solar Alliance: India and the Global Climate Commons

The atmosphere is the ultimate common-pool resource. Every country benefits from a stable climate, but each has an incentive to keep emitting greenhouse gases while hoping others cut back — the classic free-rider problem applied to 195 nations. The global climate, in Hardin's terms, is a commons on the largest possible scale.

India's position in this global commons is distinctive. It is the world's third-largest emitter of greenhouse gases in absolute terms, but its **per capita emissions** (about 1.9 tonnes of CO2 per person in 2023) are well below the world average (4.7 tonnes) and a fraction of American (14.4 tonnes) or Chinese (8.9 tonnes) levels. India's argument in climate negotiations has been consistent: historical responsibility for the climate problem lies with the industrialized nations that have been emitting for two centuries, and developing countries cannot be asked to sacrifice economic growth to fix a problem they did not primarily create.

Yet India has also taken an ambitious proactive stance. At the Paris Climate Conference in 2015, Prime Minister Modi and French President Hollande jointly launched the **International Solar Alliance (ISA)** — an intergovernmental organization headquartered in Gurugram, India, bringing together over 120 countries to promote solar energy. The ISA's premise is economic as much as environmental: for tropical and subtropical countries with abundant sunlight, solar energy is now **cheaper** than coal-fired power in most conditions. The price of solar energy in India fell from Rs 17.9 per unit in 2010 to Rs 2.0-2.5 per unit by 2024, making it the cheapest source of new electricity.

India's domestic solar deployment has matched the rhetoric. Installed solar capacity grew from 2.6 GW in 2014 to over 82 GW by early 2025, with a target of 280 GW of solar by 2030 as part of the 500 GW non-fossil-fuel capacity target. Major initiatives include the PM-KUSUM scheme for solar pumps (replacing diesel-powered irrigation), rooftop solar subsidies, and solar parks like the 2,245 MW Bhadla Solar Park in Rajasthan — one of the largest in the world.

!!! tip "Key Insight"
    India's climate strategy illustrates a core lesson from commons economics: solutions do not always require sacrifice. When solar becomes cheaper than coal, the incentive to adopt clean energy aligns with the incentive to generate cheap power. The commons problem is easiest to solve when doing the right thing for the planet is also the profitable thing to do — and technology, by driving down the cost of renewables, is making that alignment possible.

## Module Connections

!!! example "Module Connection: Ostrom and Governing the Commons (Module 6)"
    Every example in this essay — Van Panchayats, Chipko, JFM, groundwater, climate — maps directly to Elinor Ostrom's framework for governing common-pool resources. Ostrom's eight design principles (clear boundaries, proportional costs and benefits, collective-choice arrangements, monitoring, graduated sanctions, conflict resolution, recognition of rights, and nested governance) are visible in the Van Panchayats that work and absent in the JFM programs that fail. Module 6's concepts are not abstractions — they are the operating system of India's relationship with its natural resources.

!!! example "Module Connection: Externalities and Public Goods (Module 6)"
    Deforestation that causes flooding downstream is a **negative externality**. A farmer's borewell that depletes the shared aquifer imposes costs on every other farmer. Solar energy that reduces carbon emissions generates a **positive externality** for the entire planet. India's commons governance challenges are, at their core, externality problems — and the policy responses (community forest management, groundwater regulation, the ISA) are attempts to **internalize** those externalities.

## Key Takeaways

1. **India's commons challenge spans forests, water, and the global atmosphere** — the same logic of shared resources and individual incentives operates at the village scale and the planetary scale.
2. **Van Panchayats in Uttarakhand demonstrate that community governance of forests can outperform state management,** confirming Ostrom's insight that commons do not inevitably face tragedy.
3. **The Chipko movement showed that collective action by marginalized communities can change national policy,** overcoming the free-rider problem through shared dependence and social bonds.
4. **India's groundwater crisis is an ongoing tragedy of the commons,** driven by legal frameworks that treat a shared resource as private property and by subsidies (free electricity for pumping) that accelerate depletion.
5. **The International Solar Alliance represents India's attempt to govern the global climate commons** through technology-driven incentive alignment — making clean energy cheaper rather than relying solely on sacrifice and regulation.
