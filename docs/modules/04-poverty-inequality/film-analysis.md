## Synopsis

Jamal Malik, an eighteen-year-old orphan from the slums of Mumbai, is one question away from winning 20 million rupees on India's version of *Who Wants to Be a Millionaire?* The police suspect cheating — how could a "slumdog" from Dharavi, with no formal education, answer questions that have stumped lawyers and professors? Under interrogation, Jamal recounts his life story, and each episode from his past — a childhood in the slums, communal violence, exploitation by a criminal gang leader, years of hustling and surviving on the margins of Mumbai's informal economy — turns out to have taught him the answer to one of the show's questions.

The film interweaves three timelines: Jamal's childhood with his brother Salim and the girl Latika in the slums; his adolescence navigating the city's underground economy; and his appearance on the quiz show as a young adult. Through Jamal's journey, the film presents a panoramic view of urban India — its poverty and its aspiration, its cruelty and its resilience, its informal economy and its globalizing ambitions. The final question on the show is not about knowledge at all. It is, the film suggests, about destiny — or perhaps about the capabilities that life in poverty, against all odds, can develop.

## Economics in the Film

### Scene 1: Jamal on *Who Wants to Be a Millionaire?* — Social Mobility

**Economic Concepts:** Economic mobility, human capital, inequality of opportunity

The framing device of the film — a slum-dweller competing on a quiz show watched by millions — is a parable of economic mobility. The quiz show offers what the formal economy does not: a path from the bottom to the top in a single leap, bypassing the barriers of education, caste, connections, and capital that normally determine life outcomes in India.

The police inspector's incredulity — "a chai wallah cannot know these answers" — captures a deep assumption about mobility in stratified societies: that knowledge, capability, and success belong to specific social classes. Jamal's answers come not from textbooks but from lived experience — the kind of knowledge the informal economy produces but formal credentials do not recognize.

!!! success "What the Film Gets Right"
    The premise captures a real tension in Indian society. Economic mobility research shows that intergenerational income elasticity in India is high — your parents' income is a strong predictor of your own. The probability of a Jamal actually existing is very low, which is precisely what makes the story compelling. The film does not pretend mobility is easy; it shows how extraordinary the circumstances must be.

!!! warning "What the Film Simplifies"
    The quiz show as a mobility mechanism is, of course, a narrative device. Real economic mobility depends on structural factors — access to quality education, functional healthcare, urban infrastructure, and labor market institutions. The film's "destiny" framing risks suggesting that individual luck or fate can substitute for systemic change.

### Scene 2: Childhood in Dharavi — The Informal Economy

**Economic Concepts:** Informal economy, poverty, child labor, survival strategies

The childhood sequences in the slum are the film's most economically rich material. Young Jamal and Salim navigate an economy that operates entirely outside formal structures. They scavenge recyclable materials from garbage dumps, work as unauthorized guides at the Taj Mahal (inventing historical "facts" for tourists), serve chai at construction sites, and eventually fall under the control of Maman, a crime boss who runs a child begging ring — an extreme and criminal end of the informal economy's spectrum.

These scenes depict the informal economy not as a single monolithic sector but as a layered hierarchy: from self-employed scavenging (the most marginal) through petty trading and unauthorized service work to organized criminal enterprise. At every level, work is performed without contracts, regulation, or protection.

!!! success "What the Film Gets Right"
    Dharavi's real economy is remarkably productive. Researchers estimate the settlement's annual economic output at over $1 billion, generated by an estimated 5,000 businesses and 15,000 single-room factories. The recycling industry alone processes 80% of Mumbai's recyclable waste. The film captures the energy and ingenuity of informal enterprise, even as it shows the precariousness and exploitation that accompany it.

!!! warning "What the Film Simplifies"
    The film's portrayal of slum life, while visually powerful, compresses the enormous diversity of Dharavi's economy into a few dramatic scenes. Real Dharavi includes skilled artisans earning decent wages, established businesses with multi-generational histories, and community organizations that provide informal social insurance. The begging ring run by Maman, while not entirely fictional — such exploitation does exist — represents the worst extreme rather than the typical experience.

### Scene 3: The Call Center — Globalization and Labor Market Segmentation

**Economic Concepts:** Globalization, labor market segmentation, service economy, education returns

In one sequence, Jamal works as a chai server at a call center — one of the emblematic workplaces of India's globalization story. The call center workers, young and English-speaking, earn salaries that place them firmly in India's new middle class. Jamal, who serves them tea, earns a fraction of their income. The two groups work in the same building but inhabit entirely different labor markets.

This scene illustrates **labor market segmentation** — the division of the labor market into distinct tiers with different wages, conditions, and mobility prospects. The call center workers possess the specific human capital (English fluency, computer literacy, tertiary education) that globalization rewards. Jamal possesses street intelligence, resilience, and multilingual ability — forms of human capital that the formal labor market does not value or credential.

!!! success "What the Film Gets Right"
    India's IT and business process outsourcing sector did create a visible new middle class, but it employs a small fraction of the total workforce. The contrast between the call center worker and the chai server within the same building is an accurate depiction of India's "dual economy" — a high-productivity, globally connected formal sector coexisting with a vast informal sector where most people actually work.

!!! warning "What the Film Simplifies"
    The film implies a clean binary between the call center elite and the informal underclass. In reality, the boundary is more porous. Many call center workers come from modest backgrounds and use their earnings to support extended families. And the informal economy itself has a wage ladder — an experienced autorickshaw driver may earn more than an entry-level call center worker. The segmentation is real but less absolute than the film suggests.

### Scene 4: Latika's Trajectory — The Gender Gap

**Economic Concepts:** Gender gap, capability deprivation, agency, economic dependence

Latika's story arc is the film's starkest illustration of how gender shapes economic outcomes. While Jamal and Salim — despite their poverty — exercise a degree of agency, making choices about where to go and what to do, Latika is repeatedly denied this agency. She is captured by Maman's gang, forced into service as a dancing girl, and later kept as a possession by the gangster Javed. Her trajectory is defined not by her choices but by the choices men make about her.

This is gender inequality operating through the **capability approach**: Latika and Jamal start with identical poverty, but her capabilities — what she is free to do and become — are far more constrained. She cannot walk through the city alone. She cannot access the informal labor markets that Jamal navigates. Her economic dependence on male "protectors" is itself a form of poverty that income measures do not capture.

!!! success "What the Film Gets Right"
    The constraints Latika faces reflect documented realities. India's female labor force participation rate is among the world's lowest. Safety concerns, mobility restrictions, and gendered social norms dramatically reduce the economic capabilities available to women — particularly poor women in urban settings. The film connects gender deprivation to poverty without reducing either issue to the other.

!!! warning "What the Film Simplifies"
    Latika is almost entirely passive — she is rescued, captured, and rescued again. Real women in similar circumstances exercise far more agency than the film allows her. Self-Employed Women's Association (SEWA) in Ahmedabad, women-led self-help groups, and female entrepreneurs in urban informal markets demonstrate that even within severe constraints, women actively shape their economic lives. The film's romantic narrative requires Latika to be saved; reality is more complex.

### Scene 5: The Final Question — Luck vs. Capability

**Economic Concepts:** Capability approach, structural inequality, meritocracy, chance

The film's climax poses its central economic question through a narrative choice. Jamal does not know the answer to the final question. He guesses — and guesses correctly. The subtitle that closes the film reads: "It is written." The implication is that destiny, not knowledge or effort, determined the outcome.

For an economist, this ending is provocative. Is Jamal's success a story of capability — the skills and knowledge he acquired through a life of hardship? Or is it a story of luck — the random chance that his particular life experiences happened to match the quiz show's questions, and that his final guess was correct? The tension between these interpretations mirrors a deep debate in development economics: how much of the difference between those who escape poverty and those who remain in it is explained by individual capability, and how much by structural inequality and sheer chance?

!!! example "Indian Parallel"
    This film IS set in India, so the "parallel" is the reality itself. But the question the film raises — can individual talent and determination overcome structural deprivation? — is directly addressed by the empirical literature on economic mobility in India. Research by economists including Raj Chetty (for the US) and Sam Asher and Paul Novosad (for India) suggests that structural factors — caste, geography, parental income — explain far more of the variation in life outcomes than individual effort. The Jamal story, while inspiring, is the exception that illuminates the rule: for the vast majority, "it is written" by the circumstances of birth, not by destiny.

## The Representation Debate

*Slumdog Millionaire* provoked a fierce debate in India that is itself economically instructive. Critics accused the film of "poverty porn" — exploiting images of Indian poverty for Western audiences and award ceremonies. Supporters argued that the film gave visibility to lives that Bollywood routinely ignored or romanticized.

The economic dimension of this debate is about **whose poverty gets represented, by whom, and for what audience**. Bollywood has historically portrayed poverty through melodrama — the noble poor who suffer with dignity, whose problems are solved by a benevolent rich person or by fate. Danny Boyle's film, for all its Hollywood polish, at least presents poverty as a structural condition with economic mechanisms (informal markets, child labor, gender deprivation) rather than as a backdrop for romance.

Yet the film's resolution — a quiz show jackpot — arguably reinforces the most dangerous myth about poverty: that escape is an individual event rather than a systemic project. The 20 million rupees Jamal wins will change his life. The structural conditions that produced his poverty — the informal economy, the absence of social protection, the gender-based violence Latika endured — remain entirely untouched. Development economics, as practiced by this module's Nobel laureates Banerjee and Duflo, is precisely about changing those structures, one rigorously tested intervention at a time.

## Discussion Questions

1. The police inspector assumes Jamal must be cheating because "a slumdog cannot know these answers." What does this assumption reveal about the relationship between poverty, perceived capability, and social stratification in India?
2. Dharavi's informal economy generates over $1 billion annually yet operates almost entirely outside formal regulation. If you were a policymaker, would you try to formalize Dharavi's economy? What would you gain, and what might you destroy?
3. Compare Jamal's and Latika's trajectories. Both start in identical poverty. Why do their paths diverge so sharply? What does this tell you about the interaction between poverty and gender?
4. The film has been criticized as "poverty porn." Is there a way to represent poverty in popular culture that is both honest about deprivation and respectful of the agency of poor people? Can you think of a film or book that achieves this balance?
5. Jamal's final answer is a guess. If he had guessed wrong, the film would still end with him as a young man with no formal education working in the informal economy. Does the ending change how you evaluate the economic arguments the film makes, or are those arguments independent of whether Jamal wins?

## Further Viewing

- **Peepli Live** (2010) — A dark comedy about farmer suicide in rural India, the media frenzy it generates, and the gap between how poverty is experienced and how it is represented. Directed by Anusha Rizvi.
- **Court** (2014) — A Marathi-language film that uses a legal case to expose the structural inequalities of caste, class, and language in the Indian justice system. Directed by Chaitanya Tamhane.
- **Lagaan** (2001) — Set in colonial India, villagers challenge their British rulers to a cricket match to escape a crushing tax. A parable about collective action, colonialism, and rural poverty. Directed by Ashutosh Gowariker.
