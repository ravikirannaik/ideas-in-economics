## Overview

Every day you make dozens of decisions without realizing you are doing economics. Should you sleep another ten minutes or get to class on time? Should you spend your pocket money on a new phone case or save it for a concert next month? Should the government build a highway or fund a hospital? These questions may seem unrelated, but they share a common thread: **scarcity** forces choices, and every choice has a cost.

This module introduces the foundational ideas that economists use to make sense of the world. You will learn what scarcity means, why opportunity cost is the single most important concept in economics, and how trade-offs shape decisions at every level — from individuals to entire nations. You will also encounter a debate at the heart of modern economics: do people make decisions rationally, as classical theory assumes, or are they predictably irrational, as behavioral economists like Richard Thaler have shown?

We begin here because economic thinking is not a collection of facts to memorize. It is a lens — a way of looking at everyday situations and asking: *What are the trade-offs? Who gains? Who loses? And what incentives are shaping behavior?*

## Scarcity: The Starting Point

Economics begins with a simple observation: we cannot have everything we want. Resources — time, money, land, labor, raw materials — are finite. Wants are not. This gap between what we have and what we desire is called **scarcity**, and it is the reason economics exists as a discipline.

Scarcity is not the same as poverty. A billionaire faces scarcity of time. India, the world's fifth-largest economy, faces scarcity of clean water in many regions. Even a country with abundant natural resources must decide how to allocate them: more spending on defense means less on education; more land for factories means less for farming.

!!! example "Indian Context"
    Consider water allocation in Chennai. In 2019, the city's four major reservoirs nearly ran dry, affecting 4.6 million residents. The government had to decide: prioritize drinking water for households, or continue supplying water to IT parks that generate employment and tax revenue? This is scarcity in action — not a theoretical exercise but a daily reality that shapes policy and lives.

## Opportunity Cost: The Price of Every Choice

If scarcity forces choices, then every choice has a price — not necessarily in rupees, but in what you give up. This is **opportunity cost**: the value of the next-best alternative that you forgo when you make a decision.

Suppose you have a free Saturday afternoon. You could study for your mid-term, go to a movie with friends, or pick up a shift at your part-time job that pays ₹500. If you choose to study, the opportunity cost is not the movie *and* the job. It is whichever of those two you value more — say, the ₹500 from the job. Opportunity cost is always about the single best alternative you sacrificed.

This concept is deceptively powerful. It explains why a highly paid lawyer should not spend her afternoon fixing a leaky tap even if she is good at plumbing — the opportunity cost of her time (billable legal hours) is too high. It explains why countries trade with each other even when one country is more efficient at producing everything (the principle of comparative advantage). And it reveals hidden costs that sticker prices do not show: the "free" government scheme that is financed by taxpayers, the "free" social media app that is paid for with your attention and data.

!!! tip "Key Insight"
    Opportunity cost is not about money. It is about alternatives. Whenever you hear "free," ask yourself: free for whom, and at what cost?

## Trade-offs and Marginal Thinking

Once you accept that choices have costs, the next question is: *how much?* Economists answer this using **marginal thinking** — analyzing decisions one unit at a time.

Should you study one more hour for your exam? The answer depends on the **marginal benefit** (how much your grade improves) versus the **marginal cost** (what you give up — sleep, relaxation, socializing). If the marginal benefit exceeds the marginal cost, the extra hour is worth it. If not, stop studying and do something else.

This "one more unit" logic applies everywhere. A firm decides whether to hire one more worker by comparing the additional revenue that worker generates against their wage. A government decides whether to build one more kilometer of highway by comparing the economic benefits against the construction cost. Marginal thinking is the economist's scalpel — it cuts through the noise of total costs and total benefits to focus on the decision at hand.

**The sunk cost trap.** One common mistake is letting past costs influence future decisions. If you have already spent ₹300 on a terrible movie ticket, should you stay to the end? Economically, no — the ₹300 is gone regardless. Your decision now should depend only on whether staying is better than leaving. Yet people routinely stay, eat food they have already paid for even when full, and continue failing projects because they have "invested too much to quit." This is the **sunk cost fallacy**, and recognizing it is one of the most practically useful things economics teaches.

## The Production Possibilities Frontier

The **Production Possibilities Frontier (PPF)** is economists' simplest model of scarcity and trade-offs. Imagine a country that produces only two goods — say, wheat and steel. Given its current resources and technology, there is a maximum amount of wheat it can produce if it makes no steel, and a maximum amount of steel if it makes no wheat. The curved line connecting these extremes is the PPF.

Points on the frontier represent **efficient** production — all resources are fully employed. Points inside the frontier represent **inefficiency** — the country could produce more of both goods. Points outside the frontier are currently **unattainable** without more resources or better technology.

The PPF makes trade-offs visible. Moving along the frontier from more wheat to more steel means giving up increasing amounts of wheat for each additional ton of steel. This is the principle of **increasing opportunity cost**, and it explains why the frontier is curved rather than straight.

!!! example "Indian Context"
    India's annual budget is a real-world PPF exercise. In the Union Budget 2024-25, the government allocated ₹11.1 lakh crore to capital expenditure (infrastructure) and ₹5.4 lakh crore to subsidies (food, fertilizer, fuel). Every additional rupee spent on roads and railways means one less rupee for subsidies that support the poor — unless the economy grows, pushing the frontier outward.

## Positive vs. Normative Economics

Economists make two kinds of statements, and confusing them is one of the most common mistakes in public debate.

**Positive statements** are about facts — claims that can be tested with evidence. "India's unemployment rate was 3.2% in 2024" is a positive statement. You can verify it by checking the Periodic Labour Force Survey data. It might be wrong, but it is testable.

**Normative statements** are about values — claims about what should be. "The government should raise the minimum wage" is a normative statement. It depends on what you value: more income for low-wage workers, or fewer jobs if employers cut hiring?

Good economic analysis keeps these apart. A policy debate that mixes positive claims ("minimum wage increases cause unemployment") with normative claims ("workers deserve a living wage") without distinguishing them will generate more heat than light. This course trains you to spot the difference.

## Rational Choice and Its Limits

Classical economics rests on the assumption of **rational choice**: people have clear preferences, weigh costs and benefits, and choose the option that maximizes their well-being. This is a powerful simplification. It allows economists to build models that predict market behavior, trade patterns, and policy effects with surprising accuracy.

But do people actually behave this way? In the 1970s, psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky began documenting systematic patterns in how humans deviate from rational choice. People overvalue what they already own (the **endowment effect**). They are more motivated by avoiding losses than by achieving equivalent gains (**loss aversion**). They anchor their judgments on irrelevant numbers (**anchoring bias**). They make different choices depending on how a problem is framed, even when the underlying facts are identical (**framing effect**).

These are not random errors. They are **predictable** biases that follow regular patterns — which is what makes them so useful for economists. If irrationality were random, it would average out across millions of people and markets would still behave as if people were rational. But because the biases are systematic, they can move entire markets in predictable directions — creating bubbles, panics, and persistent mispricings.

This is where **behavioral economics** enters the picture, and where our module's Nobel laureate, Richard Thaler, made his greatest contribution.

## Market Bubbles and Financial Crises

A **market bubble** occurs when the price of an asset — houses, stocks, tulips — rises far above its fundamental value, driven by speculation and herd behavior rather than rational assessment.

Bubbles share a common pattern. Early investors buy an asset because they believe it is undervalued. Prices rise. Others see prices rising and buy in, not because they have analyzed the fundamentals, but because they expect prices to keep rising. Media attention attracts more buyers. A self-reinforcing cycle pushes prices to absurd levels. Eventually, something triggers a reassessment — a default, a policy change, a shift in sentiment. Prices collapse. Investors who bought at the peak are wiped out. If the bubble is large enough, the collapse spreads through the financial system and becomes a **financial crisis**.

The 2008 global financial crisis — the subject of this module's film, *The Big Short* — followed this pattern almost textbook-perfectly. American banks made increasingly risky mortgage loans to borrowers who could not afford them, then bundled these loans into complex financial products and sold them to investors worldwide. When housing prices stopped rising and borrowers defaulted, the entire structure collapsed, triggering the worst global recession since the 1930s.

!!! example "Indian Context"
    India experienced its own version in the IL&FS crisis of 2018. Infrastructure Leasing & Financial Services, a major non-banking financial company (NBFC), defaulted on its debt obligations, triggering a liquidity crisis across India's NBFC sector. The event exposed weaknesses in credit rating agencies, shadow banking regulation, and the interconnectedness of India's financial system — themes that parallel the 2008 crisis.

## Connecting to the Nobel Laureate

!!! nobel "Nobel Connection: Richard Thaler (2017)"
    Richard Thaler's career has been dedicated to showing that the gap between how economists assume people behave and how they actually behave is not a minor detail but a central feature of economic life. His concept of **nudges** — small changes in how choices are presented that steer people toward better decisions without restricting their freedom — has reshaped public policy worldwide. In India, the design of the Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) system and Swachh Bharat campaign both draw on insights from behavioral economics.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Scarcity** is the fundamental economic problem — it exists for all societies, regardless of wealth.
2. **Opportunity cost** is the true cost of any decision: the best alternative you give up.
3. **Marginal thinking** asks "one more?" rather than "all or nothing?" — it is the economist's most practical tool.
4. **Sunk costs** should not influence future decisions, but the sunk cost fallacy trips people up constantly.
5. The **Production Possibilities Frontier** makes trade-offs and efficiency visible in a simple model.
6. **Positive** economics describes what is; **normative** economics argues what ought to be. Keep them separate.
7. **Rational choice** is a useful assumption, but real people exhibit systematic biases.
8. **Behavioral economics** studies these biases and uses them to design better policies.
9. **Market bubbles** arise when herd behavior and speculation overwhelm rational pricing.
10. **Financial crises** result when bubbles burst and the damage spreads through an interconnected system.
