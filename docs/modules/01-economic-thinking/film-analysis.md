## Synopsis

In the mid-2000s, the American housing market is booming. Banks are issuing mortgages to anyone with a pulse, bundling those mortgages into financial products called collateralized debt obligations (CDOs), and selling them to investors worldwide. Credit rating agencies stamp these products as safe. Regulators look the other way. The entire system operates on the assumption that housing prices will keep rising.

A handful of outsiders see what almost nobody else does: the foundation is rotten. Michael Burry, a socially awkward hedge fund manager with a glass eye, discovers that the mortgages underlying these products are defaulting at alarming rates. Jared Vennett, a Deutsche Bank trader, stumbles onto Burry's research and sees a profit opportunity. Mark Baum, an idealistic fund manager, investigates the mortgage market on the ground in Florida and is horrified by what he finds. Two young investors, Charlie Geller and Jamie Shipley, realize from a Colorado garage that they can bet against the entire housing market.

Each of them "shorts" the housing market — bets that it will collapse. They are ridiculed, ignored, and pressured to sell their positions. For two years, the market keeps rising. Then, in 2007-2008, the bubble bursts. The housing market collapses. Banks fail. Millions lose their homes. The global economy enters its worst recession since the Great Depression. The outsiders who bet against the market make billions — but the film makes clear that their victory tastes bitter, because the cost is borne by ordinary people who had no say in the decisions that destroyed their savings and jobs.

## Economics in the Film

### Scene 1: Michael Burry Reads the Data

**Economic Concepts:** Rational analysis, information asymmetry, opportunity cost of ignoring data

Burry sits in his office at Scion Capital, poring over thousands of individual mortgage loan documents. He discovers that a growing share of mortgages are "subprime" — issued to borrowers with poor credit histories, adjustable interest rates, and little ability to repay once the low introductory rate expires. Yet these mortgages are packaged into CDOs rated AAA (the safest category) by credit rating agencies.

Burry's behavior is the textbook definition of rational economic analysis: he gathers data, evaluates fundamentals, and makes a decision based on evidence. Everyone around him is doing the opposite — relying on the assumption that housing prices always go up, a belief with no logical foundation.

!!! success "What the Film Gets Right"
    The real Michael Burry did exactly this. His Scion Capital letters from 2005-2006, publicly available, document his analysis in painstaking detail. The film accurately portrays both his reasoning and the disbelief he encountered from banks, investors, and his own clients.

!!! warning "What the Film Simplifies"
    The film presents Burry as almost alone in spotting the bubble. In reality, several analysts at investment banks and rating agencies raised internal warnings that were ignored. The problem was not that nobody knew — it was that the incentive structure rewarded short-term profits over long-term risk assessment.

---

### Scene 2: The CDO Explained (Margot Robbie in a Bathtub)

**Economic Concepts:** Economic models, complexity as a feature, information asymmetry

The film pauses its narrative to have the actor Margot Robbie, sitting in a bathtub sipping champagne, explain what a CDO is in plain English. She describes how banks take thousands of individual mortgages — many of them risky — bundle them together, and slice the bundle into tranches. The top tranche gets paid first and is rated safest. The bottom tranche absorbs losses first and is rated riskiest. The magic trick: by bundling, the aggregate product is rated safer than its individual components.

This scene illustrates a key insight about economic models. The mathematical models used to rate CDOs assumed that mortgage defaults were largely independent — that one borrower defaulting did not make it more likely that their neighbor would also default. This assumption was wrong. When housing prices fell across the country simultaneously, defaults were highly correlated, and the "safe" top tranches turned out to be just as toxic as the bottom ones.

!!! success "What the Film Gets Right"
    The CDO structure worked exactly as described. The diversification assumption was the critical flaw, and rating agencies applied it mechanically without questioning whether it held in a nationwide housing downturn.

!!! warning "What the Film Simplifies"
    CDOs were more complex than the film suggests — there were CDOs made of other CDOs (called "CDO-squared"), synthetic CDOs that referenced mortgages without actually owning them, and multiple layers of financial engineering. The film wisely simplifies to communicate the core mechanism.

### Scene 3: Mark Baum Visits Florida

**Economic Concepts:** Market bubble, incentives, moral hazard, on-the-ground empirics

Mark Baum and his team travel to Florida, where the housing boom is in full swing. They visit newly built neighborhoods where houses sit empty. They talk to a mortgage broker who cheerfully admits he issues loans without checking income ("stated income" or "liar's loans"). They meet a stripper who owns five houses and a condo, all purchased with adjustable-rate mortgages she cannot afford once the interest rate resets.

This is one of the film's most effective sequences because it shows how **incentives** drive behavior. The mortgage broker earns a commission for every loan issued, regardless of whether the borrower can repay. The bank earns fees by packaging the loans and selling them to investors. The rating agency earns fees from the bank for rating the CDO. At no point in this chain does anyone bear the cost of a default — that cost is passed along to investors at the end of the line. This separation of risk from reward is **moral hazard**: when someone benefits from taking risks but does not bear the consequences, they will take excessive risks.

!!! success "What the Film Gets Right"
    The "liar's loan" problem was real and well-documented. A 2006 study by the Mortgage Asset Research Institute found that 60% of stated-income loans contained exaggerated income figures. The incentive structure was exactly as the film portrays.

!!! warning "What the Film Simplifies"
    The film implies that brokers and bankers were uniformly cynical. Many genuinely believed housing prices would keep rising and that their products were safe. The behavioral economics of the bubble — groupthink, confirmation bias, the difficulty of challenging a consensus — is as important as the incentive misalignment.

---

### Scene 4: The Rating Agencies

**Economic Concepts:** Conflict of interest, information asymmetry, regulatory failure

In a pivotal scene, Mark Baum visits a credit rating agency (based on Standard & Poor's) and confronts an analyst about why clearly risky CDOs receive AAA ratings. The analyst admits, with discomfort, that if her agency refuses to rate a product favorably, the bank will simply take its business to a competitor. The rating agencies were paid by the banks whose products they were supposed to evaluate objectively — a textbook conflict of interest.

This scene illustrates what happens when the incentive structure of a market undermines the information system that market participants rely on. Investors trusted AAA ratings because they had historically been reliable. But the agencies had shifted from a model where investors paid for ratings (aligning incentives with accuracy) to one where banks paid (aligning incentives with leniency).

---

### Scene 5: The Collapse

**Economic Concepts:** Financial crisis, systemic risk, sunk cost fallacy, moral hazard revisited

As defaults mount in 2007, the outsiders expect immediate vindication. Instead, the banks initially refuse to acknowledge losses, and the prices of their short positions do not rise. The banks are engaged in a massive sunk cost fallacy — they have invested so heavily in the housing market that they cannot psychologically or financially admit it is failing. They double down, hoping the problem will pass.

When the collapse finally comes in 2008, it is catastrophic. Bear Stearns fails. Lehman Brothers collapses. AIG, which had insured many of the toxic CDOs, requires a $182 billion government bailout. The U.S. government decides that some banks are "too big to fail" — their collapse would destroy the entire financial system — and pumps hundreds of billions into the banking sector. The moral hazard is complete: the people who took the risks are rescued; the people who lost their homes and jobs are not.

## The Economics Behind the Story

The 2008 financial crisis was not a natural disaster. It was the result of a market system that functioned exactly as its incentive structure dictated. Brokers were incentivized to issue bad loans. Banks were incentivized to package and sell them. Rating agencies were incentivized to approve them. Regulators were under political pressure to promote homeownership and light-touch supervision. Investors were incentivized to chase yield in a low-interest-rate environment.

At every link in the chain, individual actors were responding rationally to the incentives they faced. The problem was that the incentives were misaligned with the collective good. This is a profound lesson about markets: they are extraordinarily powerful at coordinating behavior, but the behavior they coordinate depends entirely on the rules of the game.

!!! example "Indian Parallel"
    India's IL&FS crisis of 2018 shares several structural features with the 2008 crisis. IL&FS, a systemically important non-banking financial company, borrowed short-term money to finance long-term infrastructure projects — a classic maturity mismatch. Credit rating agencies maintained high ratings until weeks before the default. The contagion spread through the NBFC sector, tightening credit for small businesses and consumers. The government intervened by superseding IL&FS's board, echoing the "too big to fail" logic. The crisis exposed gaps in the regulation of India's shadow banking system that are still being addressed.

## Discussion Questions

1. Michael Burry's bet against the housing market was rational, yet he faced enormous pressure from his investors to abandon it. Why is it so hard to act against the consensus, even when the evidence supports your position?
2. The mortgage broker in Florida earns a commission regardless of whether the borrower repays. Redesign the incentive structure to align the broker's interests with the long-term health of the loan. What trade-offs does your design create?
3. After the crisis, no senior bank executive was criminally prosecuted. Using the distinction between positive and normative economics, separate the factual question (did they break the law?) from the values question (should the laws have been different?).
4. Compare the 2008 US housing crisis with India's IL&FS crisis. What structural features do they share? What is different about the Indian institutional context?
5. The film's tagline is "This is a true story." To what extent can a Hollywood film be a reliable source for understanding economic events? What are its advantages and limitations compared to an academic paper or a data visualization?
